Market Movers: Five Years of Stock & ETF Gainers

[Your Name Here] · Financial Data Analytics

This report covers monthly price and volume data for 7,681 U.S. stocks and ETFs from January 2015 through December 2019 — 350,243 ticker-months in total. I aggregated raw daily prices into monthly open/high/low/close/volume records for every ticker, then looked at which tickers, exchanges, and years produced the biggest gains and losses. The headline findings: the market's two sharpest single-month drops both landed in a fifteen-month window bracketing the late-2018 selloff, the very next month (January 2019) was the best month in the entire five years, ETFs delivered smoother and slightly higher average monthly returns than individual stocks, and the biggest single-month gainers were almost entirely small biotech and growth names.

1. Five years, one long climb with two sharp air-pockets

Averaging the monthly return of every ticker in the data shows a market that was positive more often than not, but with two unmistakable drawdowns. The worst month in the whole period was December 2018 at −10.1%, part of the broader Q4 2018 selloff that also took October 2018 down −8.3%. The rebound was just as sharp: January 2019 was the single best month, at +10.2% — the market's best and worst months sit only 31 days apart.

2. 2018 was the market's worst year; 2019 its best

Rolling the monthly numbers up to calendar years, 2018 was the only clearly negative year at −1.33% average monthly return, driven almost entirely by that October–December stretch. 2019 was the strongest year at +1.16%, largely on the back of January's rebound. 2016 and 2017 were both modestly positive (+0.96% and +0.70%), while 2015 was slightly negative (−0.72%).

3. The biggest single-month gainers were almost all small biotech and growth stocks

Ranking every ticker-month by return, the single biggest gain in the data was Zogenix (ZGNX), which rose 193.3% in September 2017 on positive trial data for an epilepsy drug. Eight of the top ten single-month gainers are small-cap biotech or specialty-pharma names (Zogenix, Veritone, Pennant Group, My Size, X4 Pharmaceuticals, Capricor, BioCardia, Relmada). The one instantly recognizable name is Tilray (TLRY), up 176.5% in August 2018 during the run-up in cannabis-sector stocks shortly after its IPO.

4. The steepest single-month drops cluster around small, thinly-traded names

The mirror image holds on the downside: the ten steepest single-month declines all belong to small or thinly-traded tickers such as Eagle Point Income (EIC), China Index Holdings (CIH), and Intercorp Financial Services (IFS), several losing more than 95% of their value in a single month. A number of these coincide with corporate actions — reverse splits, SPAC-related reclassifications, or ADR ratio changes — that can produce an extreme reported return without necessarily reflecting a pure trading loss; see the data notes below for how these were screened.

5. ETFs out-gained individual stocks on average, with less noise

Across the whole period, the 94,716 ETF ticker-months averaged +0.28% per month, versus +0.12% for the 255,371 individual stock ticker-months — ETFs came out ahead by more than double. That fits the diversification story: a single ETF smooths out the wild single-month swings that drive the top-gainer and top-loser lists above, so the average tilts positive even without any single spectacular winner.

6. Where a stock lists lines up with how it performed

Breaking average return down by listing exchange, NYSE American — home to a lot of small and micro-cap listings — was the only exchange with a negative average return (−0.81%). The other four venues were all positive: Cboe BZX led at +0.34%, followed by NYSE Arca (+0.24%), NYSE (+0.19%), and Nasdaq (+0.13%). This lines up with exchange listing standards — the larger, more established exchanges skew toward larger, steadier companies.

7. A handful of tickers were positive in 4 out of every 5 months

Looking only at tickers with at least 48 of the 60 months present, and ranking by the share of months with a positive return, the top of the list is dominated by short-term bond and cash-management ETFs (NEAR, MINT, SHV, FLOT) — positive 76–85% of months, which makes sense for funds designed to barely move. The interesting exception is Copart (CPRT), an ordinary common stock that was positive in 76.7% of months with a +2.79% average monthly return, making it the most consistent outright grower in the data, alongside waste-hauler Waste Connections (WCN).

8. Trading activity concentrates in a small set of giant, familiar names

Ranking tickers by average monthly trading volume tells a very different story from the return-based rankings above: the most actively traded ticker in the data was SPY, the S&P 500 ETF, at just under 2 billion shares a month on average, followed by Bank of America (BAC) and AMD. Six of the top ten are either broad-market ETFs (SPY, EEM, XLF, GDX) or mega-cap stocks (BAC, GE, F, AAPL) — the names everyone already knows, not the small caps that dominate the gainer/loser lists.

About this data

The source is the Stock Market Dataset by jacksoncrow on Kaggle — daily historical open/high/low/close/volume prices for U.S. stocks and ETFs, plus a NASDAQ/NYSE ticker metadata file. One row in the working dataset is one ticker in one calendar month: daily prices were aggregated into monthly open (first trading day's open), close (last trading day's close), high/low (the month's extremes), and volume (the month's total), for January 2015 through December 2019.

Rows dropped
From the ~397,000 raw ticker-months, rows were dropped if: the ticker had fewer than 15 trading days that month (partial listing/delisting months); the month's opening price was below $5 or above $500 (penny stocks and a handful of implausibly high per-share prices for obscure tickers are the most noise-prone); the computed return was beyond ±200% (±60% for ETFs, since a diversified fund realistically cannot move that much in a month) — in this dataset, moves beyond that threshold were essentially always stock splits, ticker reassignment, or other data artifacts rather than real trading, most visibly a handful of tickers showing million-percent "gains." About 12% of ticker-months were dropped by these rules, leaving 350,243.
How the numbers are computed
ReturnPct = (MonthClose − MonthOpen) / MonthOpen × 100. Every "average return" figure above is the simple (equal-weighted) mean of ReturnPct across the ticker-months in that group — a $50B company and a $50M company count equally. "Average monthly volume" for a ticker is the mean of that ticker's MonthVolume across the months it appears. The reproduction scripts are in scripts/.
