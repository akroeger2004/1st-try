Market Movers: Stock & ETF Gainers, 2015–Present

[Your Name Here] · Financial Data Analytics

This report covers monthly price and volume data for 7,681 U.S. stocks and ETFs, spanning January 2015 through September 2026 — 414,608 ticker-months in total. The dataset blends two sources: the full universe of 7,681 tickers for 2015–2019, and the 1,000 highest-volume tickers from that same universe tracked forward into 2020–present. I aggregated raw daily prices into monthly open/high/low/close/volume records, then looked at which tickers, exchanges, eras, and years produced the biggest gains and losses. The headline findings: the COVID crash of March 2020 was more than twice as severe as the previous worst month on record, the rebound started almost immediately, the market's post-2020 leaders looked very different from its 2015–2019 leaders (Nvidia went from a $6 stock to the most actively traded ticker in the data), and about a fifth of the original top-volume names from 2015–2019 were gone by 2020 — acquired, delisted, or renamed.

1. The COVID crash dwarfed everything else in eleven years of data

Averaging the monthly return of every ticker in the data across the full 2015–present span shows a market punctuated by a handful of violent months. March 2020 was by far the worst month on record, at −22.7% — more than twice as severe as the previous record holder, December 2018's −10.1%. The rebound was almost immediate: April 2020 was the single best month in the entire dataset, at +22.1%, followed by another outsized gain in November 2020 (+17.6%, the vaccine-approval rally). The 2022 rate-hike-driven selloff produced two more sharp drops (June 2022 −10.7%, September 2022 −9.1%), but nothing has come close to March 2020's crash.

2. 2020 was the market's best full year — despite starting with a crash

Rolling the monthly numbers up to calendar years, 2020 was actually the strongest year in the data at +2.27% average monthly return, thanks to the scale of the Q2–Q4 rebound outweighing the March crash. 2018 (−1.33%) and 2022 (−1.13%) are the only two clearly negative years — 2018 driven by the Q4 selloff, 2022 by the rate-hike bear market. Every other year from 2015 through the partial 2026 has been positive, including a strong 2025 (+1.62%) in the most recent full year of data.

3. The biggest single-month gainers, all-time, are almost all small biotech and growth stocks

Ranking every ticker-month by return across the full 2015–present dataset, the single biggest gain was still Zogenix (ZGNX), which rose 193.3% in September 2017 on positive trial data for an epilepsy drug — seven of the top ten are small-cap biotech or specialty-pharma names from that same 2015–2019 window. Two 2020–present names do crack the all-time top ten: Aurora Cannabis (ACB, +184.9% in November 2020) and Jumia (JMIA, +180.4% in July 2020), both swept up in pandemic-era retail-trading manias.

4. The steepest single-month drops cluster around small, thinly-traded names

The mirror image holds on the downside: the ten steepest single-month declines all belong to small or thinly-traded tickers such as Eagle Point Income (EIC), China Index Holdings (CIH), and Intercorp Financial Services (IFS), several losing more than 95% of their value in a single month. A number of these coincide with corporate actions — reverse splits, SPAC-related reclassifications, or ADR ratio changes — that can produce an extreme reported return without necessarily reflecting a pure trading loss; see the data notes below for how these were screened.

Notably, even with five more years of data added, every one of the ten steepest losses still comes from the original 2015–2019 window — none of the 1,000 highest-volume tickers tracked into 2020–present had a single month that extreme, which is itself a sign that the 2020–present slice (drawn from more established, highly-traded companies) is less prone to this kind of data noise than the long tail of small caps in the full 2015–2019 universe.

5. ETFs and individual stocks perform almost identically once you include 2020–present

Across the full 2015–present dataset, the 104,615 ETF ticker-months averaged +0.283% per month, versus +0.277% for the 309,837 individual stock ticker-months — essentially a tie. That's a real change from the 2015–2019-only picture, where ETFs had clearly outpaced stocks: once the 2020–present mega-cap-stock rally (Nvidia, Tesla, Amazon) is folded in, individual stocks caught all the way up to ETFs' smoother, diversified returns.

6. Where a stock lists lines up with how it performed

Breaking average return down by listing exchange, NYSE American — home to a lot of small and micro-cap listings — remains the only exchange with a negative average return (−0.78%). The other four venues are all positive, and the ranking shifted once 2020–present was added: NYSE now leads at +0.38% (up from third place in the 2015–2019-only view), followed by Cboe BZX (+0.28%), NYSE Arca (+0.25%), and Nasdaq (+0.23%). NYSE's rise reflects how many of the 2020–present mega-cap winners — Berkshire, Visa, Mastercard — are NYSE-listed.

7. The most consistent gainers, all-time, are index funds and mega-caps — not bond ETFs

Looking only at tickers with at least 100 of the 141 possible months present, and ranking by the share of months with a positive return, the list is now dominated by the market's biggest names rather than sleepy bond funds: SPY (66.9% of months positive), followed by Dell, Vanguard's VOO and IVV S&P 500 funds, and Nvidia (NVDA, 65.5%). Mastercard (MA) and Vanguard Total Market (VTI) round out a list that reads like a "who's who" of the current market — a very different, more recognizable set of names than the short-term bond ETFs that topped this ranking in the 2015–2019-only data.

8. Nvidia is now the single most actively traded ticker in the data

Ranking tickers by average monthly trading volume tells a very different story from the return-based rankings above, and it changed completely once 2020–present was added: Nvidia (NVDA) is now the most actively traded ticker at 4.65 billion shares a month on average — more than double second-place TQQQ (a 3x-leveraged Nasdaq ETF popular with retail traders). SPY, Tesla, and AAPL round out the top five. Six of the top ten either didn't exist as major volume drivers in 2015–2019 or have exploded in popularity since (NVDA, TQQQ, TSLA, NIO) — a direct reflection of the AI rally and the 2020–2021 retail-trading boom.

9. Even holding the same companies constant, the 2020–present era has been stronger

The 1,000 tickers tracked into 2020–present were selected as the highest-volume names of 2015–2019. Of those 1,000, only 805 still had usable trading data in 2020–present; the rest were acquired, delisted, taken private, or renamed in the intervening years. Comparing the full 2015–2019 universe (+0.16%/month) to the 2020–present survivors (+0.92%/month) overstates the difference, though, since it mixes two different populations. The fairer test holds the company set constant: those same 805 companies averaged +0.43% per month back in 2015–2019 — already well above the market-wide average, which is exactly why they had the volume to make this list — and grew to +0.92% per month in 2020–present, roughly doubling. That's a real, if more modest, era effect once survivorship bias is removed.

10. Meme stocks, cannabis, and crypto miners drove the biggest 2020–present single-month gains

Within 2020–present specifically, the single biggest month was Aurora Cannabis (ACB), +184.9% in November 2020, during a broad rally in cannabis stocks around the U.S. election. AMC Entertainment's +158.4% in May 2021 is the clearest meme-stock-era signature in the data, and Bitcoin-mining stock Riot Platforms (RIOT, +159.2% in November 2020) reflects the crypto rally of the same period. For a longer-run example outside this top-ten list: Nvidia (NVDA) itself went from a split-adjusted $5.91 in January 2020 to $220.78 by August 2026 — a roughly 37x increase — the single clearest expression of the AI rally in this dataset.

11. The stocks that actually help someone invest look nothing like the "biggest gainer" lists above

Sections 3 and 10 rank tickers by their single best month — but a spike month is usually a one-time news event, not a sign of quality: of the ten all-time biggest gainers in section 3, eight gave back 45–82% of their value within the following year. A more useful question is which companies were reliably positive in both eras — not just once. Filtering the 805 companies with substantial data in both periods (at least 48 of 60 months in 2015–2019 and 40+ months in 2020–present) and ranking by whichever era each was less consistent in produces a very different, much steadier list of roughly 100 stocks and ETFs — Nvidia (NVDA) tops it, positive in 65% of months in both periods, followed by index funds, Coca-Cola, and a mix of industrials, chipmakers, and healthcare names. The full ranked list of 100 is explorable in the dashboard; the chart below shows the top 15 individual stocks (ETFs excluded), comparing each one's own average return in 2015–2019 against 2020–present.

A few of these have real, checkable stories behind the acceleration:

Nvidia (NVDA) — already a strong performer in 2015–2019 (+4.68%/month), it kept accelerating (+5.22%/month in 2020–present) through two stock splits tied directly to the AI buildout: a 4-for-1 split in July 2021, and a 10-for-1 split on June 7, 2024, announced alongside record quarterly data-center revenue of $22.6 billion — in our data, NVDA rose +28.9% that same month (May 2024).

Quanta Services (PWR) — an electrical-infrastructure contractor most people have never heard of, it went from a modest +0.82%/month in 2015–2019 to +3.74%/month in 2020–present, the largest acceleration of any name on this list. The driver is AI data centers' power demand: Quanta's net income roughly doubled from $486 million (2021) to $905 million (2024) as it built out the grid capacity those data centers need. It isn't a one-way story, though — PWR fell −7.9% in August 2026 in our data, a reminder that even an infrastructure-boom stock isn't immune to pullbacks.

Eli Lilly (LLY) — average monthly return nearly tripled, from +1.11% to +3.01%, on the back of its GLP-1 weight-loss drugs: Mounjaro was FDA-approved May 13, 2022 (LLY +7.6% that month in our data), and Zepbound followed November 8, 2023 (+6.5% that month).

Refiners Marathon Petroleum (MPC) and Valero (VLO) both show the same pattern: Russia's February 2022 invasion of Ukraine cut off Russian crude and refined-fuel exports, sending refining margins — and these stocks — sharply higher (MPC +8.9%, VLO +20.0% in March 2022 alone). Neither move was a straight line: both gave a chunk back that June (MPC −20.1%, VLO −18.5%) as broader recession fears hit energy stocks despite still-strong margins — and both names show the identical pattern resurfacing recently, with refining margins spiking again in 2026 amid fresh Middle East and Russia supply disruptions.

For contrast, Royal Caribbean (RCL) — a name notably absent from this consistency list — shows why "consistent" and "eventually profitable" aren't the same thing: it fell −30.9% in February 2020 and another −60.5% in March 2020 as COVID shut down the cruise industry entirely (our data shows the stock bottoming intra-month after opening at $81.50), then rebounded +54.4% the very next month. It's a genuine long-term winner from that low, but the round trip was violent enough to keep it out of the "consistent in both eras" ranking above — consistency and a good outcome are two different things.

About this data

This dataset blends two sources. 2015–2019 comes from the Stock Market Dataset by jacksoncrow on Kaggle — daily historical open/high/low/close/volume prices for all 7,681 U.S. stocks and ETFs in that file, plus a ticker metadata file. 2020–present comes from Yahoo Finance's public historical-data endpoint, fetched for the 1,000 tickers with the highest average monthly trading volume in the 2015–2019 data — a fixed, published list derived entirely from the Kaggle file, not cherry-picked after the fact. One row is one ticker in one calendar month, tagged with an Era column (2015-2019 or 2020-present) so the two sources stay distinguishable. Monthly figures are open (first trading day's open), close (last trading day's close), high/low (the month's extremes), and volume (the month's total).

Rows dropped
2015–2019: from ~397,000 raw ticker-months, rows were dropped for fewer than 15 trading days that month, an opening price below $5 or above $500, or a computed return beyond ±200% (±60% for ETFs) — in this source, moves beyond that threshold were almost always stock splits, ticker reassignment, or other data artifacts, most visibly a handful of tickers showing million-percent "gains." 2020–present: from 1,000 fetched tickers, 195 returned no usable data (delisted, acquired, or renamed since 2015–2019) and a further ~460 individual ticker-months were dropped for a null, non-positive, or implausible price (the same ±200%/±60% return rule, plus a minimum $1 open/high/low/close to exclude a small number of zero-price glitches in the raw feed — one, Agnico Eagle Mines in May 2021, would otherwise have shown a false "−100%" month). Combined, about 12% of raw ticker-months are dropped, leaving 414,608.
Known limitation
The 2020–present slice can only show tickers that were already among 2015–2019's top 1,000 by volume — it cannot surface a company that IPO'd after 2019 (for example, most 2021-era SPACs) becoming a top mover, since it was never a candidate for inclusion. Findings above that compare "all-time" rankings are only as complete as that fixed list.
How the numbers are computed
ReturnPct = (MonthClose − MonthOpen) / MonthOpen × 100. Every "average return" figure above is the simple (equal-weighted) mean of ReturnPct across the ticker-months in that group — a $50B company and a $50M company count equally. "Average monthly volume" for a ticker is the mean of that ticker's MonthVolume across the months it appears. The reproduction scripts are in scripts/.
How "cross-era consistency" (section 11) is computed
Limited to tickers with at least 48 of 60 possible 2015–2019 months and at least 40 of the 2020–present months present (roughly 100 stocks and ETFs qualify). For each, the share of months with a positive return is computed separately per era; the ranking score is the lower of the two shares, so a name only ranks highly if it was reliably positive in both periods, not just its better one.
